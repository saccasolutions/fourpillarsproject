*What happens when you eliminate income tax, replace Social Security with personal investment accounts, and guarantee every citizen $25,000 a year — funded by the very automation that's displacing workers?*

Six months ago, I started asking a question that wouldn't leave me alone: what does the American economy look like when AI can do most jobs?

Not "what job retraining programs should we fund" — that ship has sailed. Not "how do we slow down automation" — that's not happening either. The real question: what economic system actually works when human labor is no longer the primary source of economic output?

The answer I arrived at is called the Four Pillars framework. It is not a modest proposal. It eliminates federal income tax. It replaces Social Security with personal investment accounts seeded at birth. It provides every adult citizen a guaranteed income floor of $25,000 per year. And it funds the entire thing through a 20% consumption tax, a 50% automation tax, and restored corporate rates — with no money printing, no magical thinking, and a path to budget surplus within a decade.

I built the complete federal balance sheet. I ran Monte Carlo simulations. I stress-tested it across 17 real personas and 6 recession scenarios. I modeled the 20-year Social Security transition. I compared it to every UBI pilot, VAT implementation, and mandatory savings program worldwide.

Today I'm publishing everything — the full whitepaper, 12 supporting documents, and 5 Python models — as open source. Here's why.

## The Core Design

The framework starts from a simple premise: if automation is going to produce most of the economic value, then the tax system should tax automation's output, not human labor.

Right now, approximately 84% of federal revenue comes from taxing individuals — income tax and payroll contributions. Companies pay about 16%. This made sense when humans did all the work. It makes no sense when AI and robots are doing an increasing share.

The Four Pillars framework flips this. Individuals pay about 57% (primarily through a consumption tax, which is technically paid when you spend, not when you earn). Companies pay 37% — through employer payroll contributions, corporate income tax, the automation tax, and other business taxes.

The result for you personally: no federal income tax. No employee FICA deduction. You keep 100% of your paycheck. You pay a 20% VAT when you spend, but groceries, rent, healthcare, and education are exempt. And if you earn less than $80,000, you receive a direct payment — up to $25,000 per year — that phases out gradually as your income rises.

## The Numbers That Matter

I built the complete federal balance sheet. All outflows, all revenue, nothing hidden.

Total federal spending under the framework: $9.82 trillion. That includes $4.62 trillion for the income floor, $1.55 trillion for healthcare coverage, $800 billion for the Creative/Civic/Family pillar payments, and $2.80 trillion for everything else the federal government does (defense, debt service, courts, agencies).

Total revenue: $7.69 trillion. The 20% VAT generates $3.76 trillion. The automation tax generates $710 billion at current displacement levels. Employer payroll contributions at 9% with no wage cap generate $990 billion. Corporate tax at 28% generates $840 billion. A 25% investment income tax generates $410 billion. And a collection of smaller mechanisms — financial transaction tax, corporate minimum tax, wealth tax, digital advertising tax, buyback tax, estate tax reform — generates the rest.

The gap: $2.13 trillion. That's $830 billion more than the current federal deficit of $1.30 trillion. Not great. But here's the thing: the current deficit has no structural path to improvement. This one does.

The automation tax is designed to grow. As AI displaces more human labor — from 10% of tasks today to an estimated 30% over the next decade — automation tax revenue grows from $710 billion to $2.84 trillion. That $2.13 trillion increase alone closes the gap. Add GDP growth from 258 million adults with guaranteed purchasing power, and the system reaches surplus.

I didn't trust this trajectory on paper, so I ran a Monte Carlo simulation — 2,000 iterations with randomized GDP growth, recession shocks, variable automation rates, volatile investment income, and fluctuating healthcare costs. The result: median budget gap narrows from -$1.97T in Year 1 to -$0.55T by Year 10, with a 56% probability of surplus. At the 90th percentile, surplus arrives by Year 6.

The worst case — 10th percentile, bad luck all around — shows a Year 10 gap of -$1.75T. That's larger than today's deficit but not catastrophically so, and it's *still structurally better* than the current trajectory because the automation tax growth engine exists. The current system doesn't have one.

## What It Means For Real People

I stress-tested 17 personas. Every single one comes out ahead.

An unemployed adult goes from $0 to $21,850 (floor minus VAT). A single mom earning $28,000 with two kids goes from $22,960 to $68,500. A median worker at $45,000 goes from $34,050 to $55,500. A software engineer at $95,000 goes from $69,440 to $85,690. An executive at $250,000 goes from $170,861 to $229,000.

The pattern holds across the board because the combination of eliminated income tax, eliminated FICA, and the floor payment more than offsets the VAT for every income level. The gains are largest at the bottom — which is the point.

## Retirement: The $1 Million Minimum Wage Worker

This is the part that surprises people most.

Social Security is a pay-as-you-go transfer. Your payroll taxes don't go into an account with your name on it — they go to current retirees. When it's your turn, you depend on future workers paying in. With birth rates declining and life expectancy rising, the math is broken.

The framework replaces this with a mandatory 10% contribution to a 401(k)-style account that you personally own. A 22-year-old minimum wage worker contributing $2,500 per year (growing 2% annually with wage growth), at a 7% average return over 43 years, accumulates $1.04 million by retirement. At a 4% withdrawal rate, that's $41,600 per year in retirement income — plus the $25,000 floor. Total: $66,600 per year.

Social Security would have given that same worker about $16,200 per year. The 401(k) beats it by 4x.

And it gets better. The framework includes child seed accounts — government-funded investment accounts established at birth. A $5,000 seed with $1,000 annual additions through age 18, at 7% average return, grows to $51,000 by age 18, $82,000 by age 25, and $1.22 million by age 65. Combined with the 401(k), a minimum wage worker could retire with over $2 million in personal assets.

These accounts are *yours*. Unlike Social Security, they can be inherited. For the first time in American history, every citizen would build intergenerational wealth — not just the families who were already wealthy.

## Why This Isn't Inflationary

The objection I hear most: "Giving everyone $25,000 will just cause prices to go up by $25,000."

This confuses redistribution with money printing. The COVID stimulus caused inflation because it was literal monetary expansion — $5+ trillion conjured into existence through Federal Reserve bond purchases. The money supply increased. More dollars chased the same goods. Prices rose.

The Four Pillars framework is tax-funded redistribution. Every dollar paid in floor payments is collected from somewhere else — VAT on spending, automation tax on corporate savings, employer payroll contributions. The money supply doesn't change. Purchasing power shifts from corporations and high-income households to lower-income households.

Short-term inflation of 3-5% above baseline is expected for 12-24 months as spending patterns adjust. The VAT creates a one-time price level adjustment (not ongoing inflation — the UK introduced 17.5% VAT in 1991 and CPI normalized within 12 months). And long-term, automation drives structural deflation that outweighs UBI-driven demand: AI reduces production costs faster than the floor increases consumer spending.
