# Expert Engagement Plan: The Four Pillars Framework
### Strategic Plan for Academic Peer Review and Expert Validation
**Author:** Rob DeMaria | **Contact:** robert@saccasolutions.com | **Date:** April 2026

---

## 1. Purpose

The Four Pillars framework proposes a comprehensive economic redesign of the American social contract: eliminating federal income tax, replacing Social Security with personal investment accounts, establishing a universal income floor funded primarily through consumption and automation taxes, and reaching budget surplus within a decade. A proposal of this scope demands rigorous academic scrutiny before it can be taken seriously in policy circles.

Expert engagement serves three critical functions. First, it subjects the framework's economic model to adversarial review by researchers whose professional reputations depend on getting the math right. The $9.82 trillion outflow and $7.69 trillion revenue figures, the automation tax growth trajectory, and the floor phase-out mechanics all need stress-testing by economists who have spent careers modeling fiscal policy. Second, academic validation transforms the framework from an interesting thought experiment into a credible policy proposal. Policymakers, journalists, and advocacy organizations look to peer-reviewed research as a threshold for seriousness. Third, the engagement process itself will strengthen the framework. Every objection an expert raises, every assumption they challenge, every scenario they model that the framework has not yet addressed makes the final product more robust.

The Four Pillars framework contains several genuinely novel mechanisms, particularly the automation tax as a self-reinforcing revenue engine and the four-pillar contribution model that redefines what society values beyond market employment. These elements have no direct precedent in the academic literature, which means they will attract both curiosity and skepticism. The goal is to channel that skepticism into productive collaboration rather than dismissal.

## 2. Target Expert Categories

The framework touches nearly every domain of public economics. Effective peer review requires assembling expertise across six distinct categories, each corresponding to a major component of the proposal.

**Labor economists** are essential for evaluating the automation tax mechanism, the labor supply effects of a guaranteed floor, and the GDP growth assumptions embedded in Pillar 1's compensation formula. The framework's central claim that automation displacement creates a self-funding revenue source needs validation from researchers who study technological unemployment, wage polarization, and the elasticity of labor demand.

**Tax policy experts** must evaluate the fiscal architecture: whether a 20% VAT with basic-goods exemptions can generate $2.6 trillion, whether the shift from income taxation to consumption taxation is regressive or progressive given the floor payment, and whether the 28% corporate tax rate and 9% employer FICA structure create competitive disadvantages or investment distortions. The complete elimination of federal income tax is the framework's most politically provocative claim, and it needs economists who can model the distributional effects.

**UBI and cash transfer researchers** bring the most directly relevant empirical background. They can evaluate whether the phased floor ($25K full below $30K earnings, tapering to zero at $80K) behaves more like a negative income tax than a traditional UBI, and whether existing pilot data on labor supply effects, health outcomes, and spending patterns support or undermine the framework's assumptions.

**Behavioral economists** are needed to assess the human dimensions: whether a guaranteed floor reduces the cognitive burden of poverty (the "scarcity bandwidth" effect), whether the four-pillar recognition model changes how people think about non-market contribution, and whether the mandatory 10% 401(k) mechanism overcomes the behavioral barriers that make voluntary retirement savings ineffective.

**Public policy scholars** evaluate implementation feasibility, political economy, and institutional design. The phased transition plan, the administrative apparatus required for automation tax assessment, and the interaction between federal floor payments and existing state-level programs all require policy expertise.

**Healthcare economists** must assess the hybrid insurance model, in which employer-provided coverage continues for those who have it while the government covers gaps for those who do not. This is neither single-payer nor the current system, and its cost and coverage implications need modeling by specialists in health insurance markets.

## 3. Target Experts

The following researchers represent priority outreach targets based on the direct relevance of their published work to components of the Four Pillars framework. Each has an established publication record, institutional credibility, and research interests that intersect meaningfully with the framework's core mechanisms.

### Labor Economics and Automation

**Daron Acemoglu** is an Institute Professor at MIT, co-director of the Stone Center on Inequality and Shaping the Future of Work, and a 2024 Nobel laureate in Economics. His research on automation and labor markets, including his 2026 paper "Automation and Polarization" in the Journal of Political Economy, provides the empirical foundation for the framework's automation tax. Acemoglu has documented that labor's share of value-added in U.S. manufacturing fell from 74 percent to 46 percent between 1981 and 2016, precisely the kind of displacement the automation tax is designed to address. His recent work with David Autor and Simon Johnson on "Building Pro-Worker Artificial Intelligence" suggests he is open to policy interventions that redirect automation's gains. He is the single most important reviewer for the automation tax mechanism.

**David Autor** is the Daniel and Gail Rubinfeld Professor of Economics at MIT and co-director of the NBER Labor Studies Program. His scholarship on job polarization, skill demands, and the labor-market impacts of technological change makes him essential for evaluating the framework's Pillar 1 (economic contribution) formula and the automation tax structure. His recent work, including "Applying AI to Extend Worker Expertise" (Hamilton Project, February 2026) and "Labor Market Consequences of Technological Change" (January 2026), demonstrates active engagement with the question of how policy should respond to AI-driven displacement. Autor's finding that at least half of workers are already using AI in their jobs provides real-time context for the framework's automation trajectory assumptions.

### Tax Policy and Fiscal Architecture

**Emmanuel Saez** is Chancellor's Professor of Tax Policy and Public Finance and Director of the Stone Center on Wealth and Income Inequality at UC Berkeley. With Thomas Piketty, he created the foundational datasets on top income shares that document the dramatic increase in U.S. inequality since 1980. His 2025 NBER working paper "How Much Tax Do US Billionaires Pay?" with Gabriel Zucman and his 2026 expert report on the California Billionaire Tax demonstrate his continued engagement with fundamental tax reform. Saez's expertise is critical for evaluating whether the framework's shift from income taxation to consumption taxation is distributionally progressive when combined with the floor payment, and whether the VAT revenue projections are realistic.

**Gabriel Zucman** is a chaired professor at the Paris School of Economics, Summer Research Professor at UC Berkeley's Goldman School of Public Policy, and director of the EU Tax Observatory. His research has shown that 8% of global financial wealth is held offshore and that U.S. billionaires effectively pay about 0.2% of their wealth in taxes. His April 2026 collaboration with Joseph Stiglitz on tax system fairness and his advocacy for a global minimum tax on billionaires indicate strong interest in fundamental tax restructuring. Zucman's expertise in tax avoidance and evasion is particularly valuable for stress-testing the VAT and corporate tax revenue projections against behavioral responses.

**N. Gregory Mankiw** is the Robert M. Beren Professor of Economics at Harvard University and a 2026 Distinguished Fellow of the American Economic Association. As a former Chair of the Council of Economic Advisers and the author of the most widely used macroeconomics textbook (now in its 12th edition), Mankiw brings both academic rigor and policy experience. His broadly center-right economic perspective makes him a valuable adversarial reviewer. If the framework's fiscal math can survive Mankiw's scrutiny, it gains significant credibility across the political spectrum. His textbook treatment of consumption taxes, fiscal multipliers, and monetary-fiscal interaction provides a common analytical language for the review process.

### UBI and Cash Transfer Research

**Hilary Hoynes** is Chancellor's Professor of Economics and Public Policy at UC Berkeley, director of the Berkeley Opportunity Lab, and recipient of the 2024 Daniel M. Holland Medal from the National Tax Association. Her paper with Jesse Rothstein, "Universal Basic Income in the United States and Advanced Countries" (Annual Review of Economics), is one of the most cited analytical frameworks for evaluating UBI proposals. Hoynes has argued that a UBI would direct much larger shares of transfers to childless, non-elderly, non-disabled households than existing programs, and much more to middle-income rather than poor households. This critique is directly relevant to the Four Pillars floor design, which addresses it through the income-based phase-out. Her review would test whether the phase-out structure successfully targets resources to those who need them most.

**Ioana Marinescu** is Associate Professor at the University of Pennsylvania School of Social Policy & Practice, with appointments in the Economics Department and Wharton School, and a research associate at the National Bureau of Economic Research. Her comprehensive research finding that "universal basic income has little to no effect on employment, and improves the health and education of the population" provides empirical support for the framework's assumptions about labor supply effects. Her 2026 research agenda focuses on AI's impact on worker displacement, directly aligning with the framework's automation tax rationale. Marinescu's December 2025 work challenging myths around UBI programs makes her a natural advocate-reviewer who can identify weaknesses before critics do.

**Jesse Rothstein** is Professor of Public Policy and Economics at UC Berkeley, holding the Carmel P. Friesen Chair in Public Policy and the David Pierpont Gardner Chair in Higher Education. His co-authored work with Hoynes on UBI evaluation frameworks and his research on the Earned Income Tax Credit, intergenerational economic mobility, and take-up of safety net benefits make him essential for evaluating the floor's phase-out structure. The Four Pillars floor functions mechanically like a negative income tax, and Rothstein's expertise in NIT-adjacent programs provides the right analytical lens.

**Karl Widerquist** is a political philosophy professor at Georgetown University-Qatar, founding editor of the journal Basic Income Studies, co-founder of the U.S. Basic Income Guarantee Network, and co-chair of the Basic Income Earth Network. The Atlantic Monthly has called him "a leader of the worldwide basic income movement." His dual doctorates in political theory (Oxford) and economics (CUNY) give him a unique interdisciplinary perspective. Widerquist's MIT Press book "Universal Basic Income" (2023) provides the conceptual vocabulary that the Four Pillars framework either extends or departs from, and his review would situate the proposal within the broader global basic income discourse.

**Juliana Bidadanure** founded the Stanford Basic Income Lab in 2017 and remains its Senior Advisor, having moved to an Associate Professorship in Philosophy at New York University in 2023. As a political philosopher of equality specializing in intergenerational justice and relational equality, she brings a normative perspective that complements the economists' empirical focus. The Stanford Basic Income Lab, now led by Sean Kline under Stanford's Center on Poverty and Inequality, has tracked dozens of UBI and guaranteed basic income programs globally, providing the most comprehensive landscape view of what has been tried and what has worked.

### Behavioral Economics and Poverty

**Abhijit Banerjee** is the Ford Foundation International Professor of Economics at MIT (also joining the University of Zurich in 2026) and a 2019 Nobel laureate in Economics for his experimental approach to alleviating global poverty. His research with Esther Duflo debunking the "lazy welfare recipient" myth, finding "very little evidence" that cash transfers are spent on vices, directly supports the Four Pillars' design philosophy of trusting recipients. Banerjee's expertise in randomized controlled trials for poverty interventions provides methodological guidance for the framework's proposed pilot programs.

**Sendhil Mullainathan** is a behavioral economist whose research on scarcity and poverty, particularly his book "Scarcity: Why Having Too Little Means So Much" (co-authored with Eldar Shafir), demonstrates that poverty reduces cognitive capacity more than going one full night without sleep. This finding is foundational to the Four Pillars' design philosophy that a guaranteed floor is not charity but cognitive infrastructure. Mullainathan's work shows that the same behavioral patterns attributed to character flaws in the poor are exhibited by anyone experiencing scarcity, which supports the framework's universal, non-means-tested approach.

### Economic Mobility and Wealth Building

**Raj Chetty** is the William A. Ackman Professor of Public Economics at Harvard University and Director of Opportunity Insights. His 2026 paper in the Quarterly Journal of Economics on growing class gaps and shrinking race gaps in economic mobility provides the empirical context for the framework's child seed accounts and four-pillar contribution model. Chetty's finding that neighborhoods are major determinants of economic outcomes connects to the framework's goal of providing a universal floor that enables geographic and economic mobility. His big-data approach to measuring mobility outcomes would be invaluable for designing the framework's measurement and evaluation systems.

**Darrick Hamilton** is University Professor, the Henry Cohen Professor of Economics and Urban Policy, and founding director of the Institute on Race, Power and Political Economy at The New School. He also serves as chief economist at the AFL-CIO. Hamilton is the intellectual architect of the "baby bonds" concept, proposing federally funded trust accounts seeded at birth and accessible in adulthood. The Four Pillars framework's child seed accounts are conceptually related to baby bonds, and Hamilton's review would clarify how the two approaches compare, where they diverge, and whether the framework's investment-account structure is superior to or complementary with the baby bonds model. His June 2025 Washington Post op-ed with Rep. Ayanna Pressley on baby bonds legislation demonstrates his continued engagement with policy implementation.

**William Darity** is the Samuel DuBois Cook Distinguished Professor Emeritus of Public Policy, African and African American Studies, and Economics at Duke University. As a founder of the field of stratification economics and co-architect of the baby bonds proposal with Hamilton, Darity brings a structural inequality perspective. His research argues that inequality arises from structural disparities in the distribution of resources and power, a framing that aligns with the Four Pillars' premise that the current economic system systematically undervalues non-market contribution. Connecticut's implementation of a baby bonds program based on Darity and Hamilton's work provides a real-world policy precedent for the framework's child seed accounts.

### Healthcare Economics

**Katherine Baicker** is the Provost of the University of Chicago, the Emmett Dedmon Distinguished Service Professor at Harris School of Public Policy, and the 2025 recipient of the William B. Graham Prize for Health Services Research. Her research on public and private health insurance, particularly the Oregon Health Insurance Experiment (a randomized evaluation of Medicaid expansion), provides the gold standard for understanding coverage effects. The Four Pillars' hybrid insurance model, in which employer coverage continues while the government covers gaps, needs Baicker's expertise to evaluate whether it avoids adverse selection, whether the government gap-coverage mechanism creates moral hazard, and whether it is fiscally sustainable.

**Amy Finkelstein** is the John & Jennie S. MacDonald Professor of Economics at MIT, a MacArthur Fellow, and recipient of the John Bates Clark Medal. Her research on market failures and government intervention in insurance markets, including her 2025 Quarterly Journal of Economics paper on the relationship between economic conditions, mortality, and welfare, makes her essential for modeling the healthcare component. Finkelstein has publicly discussed plans to restructure American health insurance, and her empirical approach to evaluating coverage mechanisms would test whether the framework's hybrid model is workable in practice.

### Policy Advocacy and Implementation

**Natalie Foster** is President and co-founder of the Economic Security Project and a Senior Fellow at the Aspen Institute Future of Work Initiative. As the author of "The Guarantee: Inside the Fight for America's Next Economy," she bridges academic research and political advocacy. Foster's organization has supported guaranteed income pilots across 18 states, and her expertise in building political coalitions for economic security programs is critical for the framework's transition from academic proposal to policy reality. While not a traditional academic, her network connects to funders, policymakers, and pilot program operators who would be essential implementation partners.

## 4. Outreach Strategy

The approach to each expert must be tailored to their published positions, institutional incentives, and likely objections. A generic request for review will be ignored. A targeted engagement that demonstrates familiarity with their work and identifies precisely how their expertise applies will get a response.

### Approaching Labor Economists (Acemoglu, Autor)

Both Acemoglu and Autor are actively publishing on the policy implications of AI-driven automation, which means they are already thinking about the central problem the framework addresses. The outreach should lead with the automation tax mechanism, framing it as a formalization of the redistribution question their research raises but does not fully answer. The key talking point is that the automation tax makes displacement self-funding: the more AI replaces human labor, the larger the revenue base that supports displaced workers. This creates a dynamic equilibrium that their static models do not currently capture. The ask should be specific: review the automation tax formula, evaluate the displacement trajectory assumptions (10% current, 30% at Year 10), and assess whether the 50% rate on net labor savings preserves the incentive to automate while capturing sufficient revenue. Initial contact should be through formal academic channels, with a concise two-page summary of the automation tax mechanism and its derivation.

### Approaching Tax Policy Experts (Saez, Zucman, Mankiw)

Saez and Zucman are natural allies given their advocacy for progressive tax reform, but they may resist the framework's consumption-tax orientation. The outreach should acknowledge this tension directly and frame the question as empirical rather than ideological: given that the floor payment effectively creates a progressive transfer, does the combined system (VAT minus floor) produce a more progressive distribution than the current income tax system? The framework shifts 37% of federal revenue to companies (up from 16%), which aligns with Saez and Zucman's position that the corporate and wealthy should bear a greater share. Lead with this number.

For Mankiw, the approach should be different. Emphasize the framework's elimination of income tax distortions, its consumption-tax efficiency, and its market-based compensation structure. Mankiw has expressed interest in consumption taxes as theoretically superior to income taxes; the framework implements this at scale. The challenge for Mankiw will be the automation tax, which he may view as a distortionary intervention in capital markets. Frame the ask around this tension: Is the automation tax justified by the externality of displacement, or does it create deadweight loss that undermines the efficiency gains from eliminating income taxation?

### Approaching UBI Researchers (Hoynes, Marinescu, Rothstein, Widerquist, Bidadanure)

These researchers will immediately recognize that the Four Pillars floor, with its income-based phase-out, functions as a negative income tax rather than a traditional universal basic income. This is a feature, not a bug, and the outreach should frame it that way. The floor is universal in eligibility (90% of adults receive a payment) and mechanical in administration (no application, no means test), but targeted in magnitude. The key question for these reviewers is whether this design captures the administrative simplicity and destigmatization benefits of UBI while achieving the targeting efficiency of an NIT.

Hoynes and Rothstein have specifically criticized standard UBI proposals for directing too much to middle-income households. The framework's phase-out structure directly addresses their critique, and the outreach should reference their Annual Review paper and ask whether the $30K/$80K thresholds and 50-cent-per-dollar taper rate achieve the optimal balance. Marinescu's empirical finding of minimal employment effects from cash transfers should be cited as supporting evidence for the floor design, while asking her to evaluate whether the phased structure preserves this finding or creates marginal disincentive effects at the phase-out boundary.

Widerquist and Bidadanure provide the philosophical grounding. The Four Pillars framework's claim that it "redefines what counts as contribution" through four independent pillars aligns with Bidadanure's work on relational equality and Widerquist's argument that basic income is a matter of freedom rather than welfare. The outreach should position the four-pillar model as an attempt to operationalize the philosophical commitments they have articulated.

### Approaching Behavioral Economists (Banerjee, Mullainathan)

The framework's design philosophy, which explicitly states "no punishment, no ceiling, trust humans," is grounded in the behavioral economics research these scholars have pioneered. The outreach should frame the framework as an applied implementation of their findings: if scarcity reduces cognitive bandwidth, then a guaranteed floor is cognitive infrastructure; if cash transfers do not increase vice spending, then conditionality is administrative waste. The specific ask should focus on the mandatory 10% 401(k) mechanism, which is a paternalistic intervention in a framework that otherwise emphasizes trust. This tension is a genuine design question that behavioral economists are best equipped to resolve.

### Approaching Mobility and Wealth-Building Researchers (Chetty, Hamilton, Darity)

The child seed accounts and four-pillar contribution model directly engage with Chetty's mobility research and Hamilton and Darity's baby bonds work. The outreach should position the child seed accounts as an evolution of baby bonds, funded through the same automation tax engine that supports the floor, and ask whether the framework's investment-account structure (mandatory 10% 401(k) plus child seed accounts) creates a credible path to wealth accumulation for families currently locked out of asset ownership. Chetty's Opportunity Insights data infrastructure could be invaluable for designing the framework's measurement approach.

### Approaching Healthcare Economists (Baicker, Finkelstein)

The framework's hybrid insurance model is its least developed component, which is exactly why these experts are critical early reviewers. The outreach should be transparent about this: the framework preserves employer-provided insurance because it works for the majority of workers and avoids the political impossibility of eliminating private coverage, but it creates government gap coverage for the uninsured and underinsured. The ask is whether this hybrid model is economically coherent, whether it creates adverse selection spirals, and what design parameters are needed to make it stable. Reference Baicker's Oregon experiment and Finkelstein's work on insurance market failures as the analytical foundation.

## 5. Engagement Formats

Expert engagement should be structured across multiple formats, each serving a different purpose and requiring a different level of commitment. Not every expert will agree to every format, and the engagement plan should be designed so that even minimal participation (a single paper review) produces valuable feedback.

**Targeted Paper Review** is the entry-level engagement. Individual experts are asked to review specific sections of the framework that correspond to their expertise. Labor economists review the automation tax; tax policy experts review the fiscal architecture; UBI researchers review the floor design. The review request should include a structured response template with specific questions, not an open-ended "tell us what you think." This format works for busy senior researchers who cannot commit to ongoing involvement but are willing to spend a few hours with a focused document. Compensation in the form of a modest honorarium ($1,000 to $2,500) signals professional respect and increases response rates.

**Technical Advisory Board** is the intermediate commitment. A standing group of four to six experts who review the framework iteratively as it evolves, participate in quarterly calls, and provide guidance on research methodology and presentation. Advisory board members receive co-authorship credit on the final whitepaper's acknowledgments section and are compensated with an annual retainer. The advisory board should be balanced across disciplines and, ideally, across political orientations. Having both Saez and Mankiw on the same advisory board forces the framework to survive scrutiny from both directions.

**Co-Authorship Opportunities** represent the deepest engagement. If a reviewer identifies a component of the framework that aligns with their active research agenda, the invitation should extend to co-authoring a peer-reviewed paper on that specific mechanism. The automation tax, for example, could be developed into a standalone paper with Acemoglu or Autor. The floor phase-out mechanics could be co-authored with Hoynes or Rothstein. Co-authorship transforms the expert from reviewer to stakeholder, and a jointly authored paper in a top economics journal would provide the strongest possible academic validation.

**Conference Presentations** create public visibility and invite broader academic feedback. Target venues include the American Economic Association annual meeting, the National Tax Association conference (where Hoynes received her 2024 medal), the NBER Summer Institute, and the Basic Income Earth Network congress. Conference presentations should be structured as working paper presentations with assigned discussants, ideally drawn from the target expert list. A presentation at the AEA meetings with Acemoglu or Hoynes as a discussant would generate significant attention.

**Workshop Convening** brings multiple experts together for a focused one- or two-day workshop on the framework. This format is particularly valuable for identifying interactions between components that individual reviewers might miss. A workshop with labor economists, tax policy experts, and healthcare economists in the same room would surface questions about how the automation tax interacts with the VAT, how employer insurance mandates interact with the corporate tax, and how the floor payment interacts with healthcare utilization. Workshop costs ($15,000 to $30,000 for venue, travel, and honoraria) are modest relative to the value of concentrated multi-expert review.

## 6. Timeline

The engagement process should unfold in four phases over approximately twelve months, moving from initial outreach through iterative review to public presentation.

### Phase 1: Foundation (Months 1-3, May-July 2026)

The first three months focus on preparing materials and initiating contact. The framework must be distilled into review-ready documents before any outreach occurs. This means producing a concise technical summary (10-15 pages) of the complete framework, a detailed automation tax methodology paper (15-20 pages), a fiscal model with full assumptions and sensitivity analysis, and a two-page executive brief for initial outreach emails. During this phase, identify institutional connections. MIT affiliations (Acemoglu, Autor, Banerjee, Finkelstein) suggest a potential clustering strategy where a single institutional contact can facilitate introductions. Berkeley affiliations (Saez, Hoynes, Rothstein) offer a similar opportunity. Send initial outreach emails to all target experts by the end of Month 2, with follow-up within two weeks for non-responses. Expect a 20-30% positive response rate on first contact.

### Phase 2: Initial Review (Months 4-6, August-October 2026)

Distribute targeted review documents to experts who have agreed to participate. Each expert receives the full technical summary plus the specific component document relevant to their expertise. Allow six to eight weeks for review. Schedule individual calls with each reviewer to discuss their feedback, rather than relying solely on written comments. Written comments identify problems; conversations reveal the reasoning behind them and often suggest solutions. Compile review feedback into a structured revision plan. Identify areas of consensus (where multiple reviewers agree on a strength or weakness) and areas of disagreement (where reviewers conflict, indicating genuine uncertainty that the framework must acknowledge).

### Phase 3: Revision and Deepening (Months 7-9, November 2026-January 2027)

Revise the framework based on Phase 2 feedback. This is where the framework either survives or requires fundamental restructuring. If the automation tax assumptions are challenged, run the revised scenarios. If the floor phase-out creates unintended marginal disincentives, redesign the taper. If the healthcare hybrid model is deemed incoherent, develop alternatives. Circulate the revised framework to the same reviewers for a second round of comment. This iterative process is what distinguishes serious academic engagement from performative validation. During this phase, formalize the advisory board from among the most engaged reviewers. Begin discussions about co-authorship on component papers. Submit a conference paper proposal to the AEA or NTA for their next annual meeting.

### Phase 4: Publication and Presentation (Months 10-12, February-April 2027)

Finalize the whitepaper incorporating all rounds of review feedback. Submit to a peer-reviewed journal, targeting outlets such as the Quarterly Journal of Economics, the Journal of Political Economy, the American Economic Review, or the Brookings Papers on Economic Activity. Prepare conference presentations. If a workshop convening has been organized, hold it during this phase to generate a final round of expert input before publication. The goal by the end of Month 12 is a submitted manuscript with documented expert review, an established advisory board, and at least one conference presentation scheduled.

## 7. Key Academic Talking Points

The Four Pillars framework contains several features that are genuinely novel in the UBI and tax policy literature. The outreach strategy should lead with these differentiators, framed in the analytical language that academic economists use.

### The No-Income-Tax Architecture

No other comprehensive UBI proposal eliminates federal income tax entirely. Most UBI proposals layer a cash transfer on top of the existing tax system, creating complex interactions between the transfer and marginal tax rates. The Four Pillars framework eliminates this complexity by funding the system through consumption tax (VAT), automation tax, and corporate tax. The academic argument is that consumption taxes are theoretically more efficient than income taxes because they do not distort the labor-leisure tradeoff or the savings-consumption tradeoff. The framework implements this theoretical preference at national scale, which has never been modeled for the United States. The question for reviewers is whether the combination of a 20% VAT (with basic-goods exemptions) and a $25K floor payment produces a more progressive effective tax schedule than the current system. Preliminary analysis suggests it does, because the floor payment functions as a large negative tax for lower-income households, more than offsetting the regressive incidence of the VAT.

### The Automation Tax as Novel Mechanism

The automation tax (50% of net labor savings from AI and robotics) has no direct precedent in the academic literature or in existing tax policy. The closest analogue is Bill Gates' 2017 suggestion of a "robot tax," but the Four Pillars framework operationalizes this by taxing net labor cost savings rather than physical robots, which captures AI-driven displacement that does not involve physical automation. The self-reinforcing dynamic is the key academic insight: as automation displaces more workers, the tax base grows, automatically increasing the revenue available to fund the floor. At 10% displacement today, the automation tax generates approximately $0.71 trillion. At 30% displacement in Year 10, it generates $2.84 trillion. This creates a fiscal system that becomes healthier as the economic disruption it addresses becomes more severe. The modeling challenge, which reviewers should be invited to address, is whether the 50% rate preserves sufficient incentive to automate, and whether the net-labor-savings calculation is administratively feasible.

### The Phased Floor as NIT Variant

The floor structure ($25K full below $30K earnings, phasing out at 50 cents per dollar above $30K, reaching zero at $80K) is mechanically a negative income tax with a generous exemption threshold. The academic lineage traces directly to Milton Friedman's NIT proposal, but with two key modifications. First, the exemption threshold is set at $30K rather than at the poverty line, which means 90% of adult citizens receive some payment. Second, the 50% taper rate is significantly more generous than most NIT proposals, which used rates of 50-70%. The framework argues that the combination of a high exemption threshold and moderate taper rate eliminates the "poverty trap" problem that plagued earlier NIT designs, because the effective marginal tax rate never exceeds 50% at any income level (there is no income tax to compound the taper). The question for UBI and NIT researchers is whether this design achieves universality in practice (through near-universal coverage) while maintaining targeting in principle (through the phase-out).

### The Four-Pillar Contribution Model

The four-pillar structure (Economic, Creative, Civic, Family) is the framework's most philosophically ambitious claim: that a society can formally recognize and compensate non-market contribution without creating a bureaucratic assessment apparatus. The Economic pillar is simply market compensation linked to GDP growth. The Creative, Civic, and Family pillars use existing institutional data (publication records, organizational memberships, school enrollment data, caregiving records) supplemented by attestation only where institutional data does not exist. This is a testable proposition: can existing administrative data identify creative, civic, and family contribution with sufficient accuracy to support compensation decisions? The question connects to Chetty's work on using administrative data for economic research, to Bidadanure's philosophical work on relational equality, and to the broader literature on measuring non-market production (household labor valuation, volunteer labor, cultural capital).

### The Company Revenue Share Shift

The framework shifts the company share of federal revenue from 16% to 37%, while eliminating income tax and employee FICA. This rebalancing is the core fiscal mechanism: companies pay more through corporate tax, employer FICA, and the automation tax, while individuals pay through the VAT but receive the floor payment. The net effect, as demonstrated in the persona stress tests, is that every income level from unemployed to executive comes out ahead after accounting for eliminated income taxes and received floor payments. The academic question is whether a 37% company share creates capital flight, reduces investment, or shifts production offshore. The framework's response is that the guaranteed purchasing power created by the floor ($25K for every adult citizen) creates domestic demand that offsets higher corporate costs, but this claim needs modeling by macroeconomists who specialize in open-economy fiscal policy.

### Path to Surplus

The framework is, to the author's knowledge, the only comprehensive UBI proposal that projects a path to budget surplus. The initial gap of $2.13 trillion ($830B more than the current deficit) closes to surplus by Year 7-10 through automation tax growth and GDP expansion from increased consumer spending. This claim rests on specific assumptions about the pace of automation, the fiscal multiplier from floor payments, and the elasticity of VAT revenue to consumption growth. Each assumption should be independently evaluated, and the sensitivity of the surplus timeline to parameter variation should be modeled. If the framework can demonstrate that surplus is achievable under pessimistic assumptions (slower automation, lower multiplier), the fiscal credibility argument becomes significantly stronger.

## Conclusion

The Four Pillars framework is a serious attempt to solve a problem that most economists agree is real: the current economic model has no mechanism for distributing the gains from automation broadly, and existing safety net programs are inadequate for a world in which AI makes large categories of human labor economically unnecessary. The framework's approach, which eliminates income tax, creates a guaranteed floor funded by consumption and automation taxes, and rewards contribution across four dimensions of human activity, is sufficiently novel to warrant rigorous academic examination.

The expert engagement plan outlined here is designed to subject every component of the framework to scrutiny by the researchers best positioned to identify its strengths and weaknesses. The goal is not validation but improvement. A framework that survives peer review by Acemoglu on automation, Saez on tax policy, Hoynes on transfer design, Baicker on healthcare, and Chetty on mobility outcomes is one that policymakers can take seriously. A framework that does not survive that review should be revised until it can, or honestly acknowledged to be insufficient.

The engagement timeline targets a twelve-month process from initial outreach to submitted manuscript, with the expectation that the framework will be substantially revised based on expert feedback. The final product should be stronger for having been challenged, and the expert relationships built through this process will be essential for the framework's transition from academic proposal to policy conversation.
