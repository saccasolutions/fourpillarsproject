## 1. GDP Attribution Methodology

### 1.1 How US GDP Is Calculated (BEA Methodology)

The Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) calculates GDP using three interlocking approaches:

**Expenditure Approach (most cited):** GDP = C + I + G + (X - M), where C = Personal consumption expenditures (~68% of GDP), I = Gross private domestic investment (~18%), G = Government consumption & investment (~17%), X - M = Net exports (~-3%).

**Production (Value-Added) Approach:** GDP = Sum of Value Added across all industries. Value Added = Gross Output - Intermediate Inputs. This is the approach most relevant to the Economic Pillar.

**Income Approach:** GDP = Compensation of Employees + Gross Operating Surplus + Mixed Income + Taxes on Production less Subsidies. Directly shows how GDP splits between labor and capital.

**Source:** BEA, "Measuring the Economy: A Primer on GDP and the National Income and Product Accounts" (bea.gov/methodologies)

## 2. Pillar 1 Formula: GDP-Linked Market Compensation

### 2.1 The Formula

```
PILLAR 1 PAYMENT = Market_Role_Compensation × GDP_Growth_Factor

Where:
  Market_Role_Compensation = what the market pays for your role
    - Employees: salary + wages + benefits (W-2 data)
    - Self-employed: business income (1099/Schedule C)
    - Gig workers: platform earnings (1099-K)
    - Athletes/performers: contract + performance bonuses
    - Government/nonprofit: total compensation (= their GDP contribution per BEA)
  
  GDP_Growth_Factor = Current_Year_GDP / Base_Year_GDP
    - Launches at 1.0 in the framework's first year
    - Ties every person's income to national prosperity
    - If GDP grows 3%, everyone's Pillar 1 grows 3%
    - If GDP contracts, Pillar 1 shrinks (but the floor holds)
```

### 2.2 Key Properties

Pillar 1 is **paid by employers, not the government.** It formalizes what already happens — market compensation — and ties it to GDP growth. This is NOT a federal expenditure. It costs the government $0.

The GDP Growth Factor serves as a tracking mechanism and aspirational target. If compensation across an industry or the economy fails to keep pace with GDP growth, the data provides early warning for policy intervention. The framework tracks the ratio but does not mandate specific wages — labor markets continue to set compensation.

**Wage suppression protection:** With a floor payment, companies might try lowering salaries. The primary defense is labor market competition. The floor actually strengthens workers' position — they can afford to walk away from bad offers. The GDP Growth Factor provides data to detect systematic wage suppression by sector.

### 2.4 Performance-Based Attribution (Athletes, Executives)

For roles with performance-based compensation, Pillar 1 naturally captures impact:

**NBA Star (DeShawn):** His Pillar 1 reflects actual GDP impact — ticket sales, broadcast revenue, merchandise, playoff performance. MVP season with a finals run → Pillar 1 ≈ $15-20M. Injured, team misses playoffs → Pillar 1 ≈ $2-4M. This already mirrors how NBA contracts work.

**Walmart CEO:** Total comp (~$27.4M) includes base salary, stock awards, performance bonuses. All counts as GDP-impact compensation. If Walmart's value added grows 5%, his Pillar 1 grows ~5%. If it shrinks, his Pillar 1 shrinks.

### 2.5 Edge Cases

**Gig workers:** Use 1099-K / platform earnings data. Platform data agreements (Uber, DoorDash, Upwork) provide verified earnings.

**Self-employed:** Schedule C income × industry value-added multiplier.

**Government/nonprofit workers:** Compensation = GDP contribution (consistent with BEA methodology). Lower Economic pillar credit offset by higher Civic pillar credit.

**Farmers/volatile income:** 3-year rolling average to smooth commodity price swings.

## 3. Automation Attribution: Ghost Salaries Fund the System

### 3.1 The Principle

When automation replaces workers, the labor cost savings are **taxed to fund UBI**, not attributed to remaining workers.

### 3.2 How It Works

**Example: Factory automation**

Before automation: 100 workers × $60K = $6M labor cost. After automation: 2 workers × $80K = $160K labor cost + $2M in technology investment.

- **The 2 remaining workers:** Pillar 1 based on THEIR role's market compensation (~$80K each). They do NOT absorb ghost salary attribution.
- **The labor cost savings:** $6M - $160K - $2M tech cost = $3.84M in net savings. Automation tax at 50% = $1.92M flowing to UBI funding.
- **The company:** Keeps $1.92M in savings — automation is still profitable.
- **The 98 displaced workers:** Each receives floor payment (up to $25K) immediately + other pillar income.
- **Who built the robots?** Engineers, manufacturers, installers — credited through their own companies' Pillar 1 contributions.

### 3.3 Why This Matters

The alternative (remaining workers absorb ghost salaries) was wrong for three reasons: (1) it wildly inflates the compensation of monitoring roles, (2) it ignores the many humans across many companies who enabled the automation, and (3) it fails to fund the system for displaced workers.

### 3.4 National Scale

Total US labor compensation: $14.1T. Current automation displacement estimated at ~10% = $1.41T in labor savings. At 50% tax rate = **$0.71T** in automation tax revenue at launch. As AI scales from 10% to 30% displacement over 10 years, this grows to **$2.1-2.8T** — the single largest growth lever in the funding model.

## 6. Healthcare Model

### 6.1 Structure

Employer-provided health insurance remains the primary coverage mechanism. The government fills gaps for populations without employer coverage.

**Current healthcare spending replaced:**
- Medicare: $0.90T → consolidated into government gap coverage
- Medicaid/CHIP: $0.60T → consolidated into government gap coverage
- Other health/welfare programs: $0.30T → consolidated
- **Total replaced: $1.80T**

**New government healthcare cost: $1.55T** — covering ~100M people (retirees, low-income, self-employed, gig workers, unemployed) at ~$15,500 per person average.

**Savings vs. current: $0.25T** (spending $1.55T to replace $1.80T in existing programs).

### 6.2 Employer Insurance Backstop

To prevent companies from dropping coverage to push workers onto the government plan:

- Companies providing qualifying insurance: **9% employer FICA rate**
- Companies NOT providing insurance: **13-14% employer FICA rate**

The surcharge exceeds the cost of providing insurance, making it cheaper to cover workers than to pay the penalty. Either way, the revenue covers those workers' healthcare.

## 7. Retirement & Investment Model

### 7.1 Mandatory 401(k)-Style Accounts

Workers contribute a mandatory 10% of earnings to personal investment accounts. With no income tax and no employee FICA, take-home pay is substantially higher even after the 401(k) contribution.

**Example — Teacher earning $65,000:**
- Annual 401(k) contribution: $6,500 (10% mandatory)
- After 38 years at 7% average return: **$1.16 million**
- 4% safe withdrawal rate: **$46,400/year**
- Plus UBI floor (full $25K at retirement): **$25,000**
- Plus Pillar 3-4 credits: ~$10,000-15,000
- **Total retirement: $81,400-$86,400/year**
- vs. current SS benefit: **$28,000-$32,000/year**

### 7.2 Child Seed Investment Accounts

Every child born to US citizen parents receives a seed investment account at birth.

| Seed Amount | Annual Add | Age 18 | Age 25 | Age 65 |
|-------------|-----------|--------|--------|--------|
| $1,000 | $500/yr | $22,700 | $43,400 | $1,072,000 |
| $5,000 | $1,000/yr | $57,400 | $107,300 | $2,591,000 |

### 7.3 SS Transition

Current Social Security recipients continue receiving whichever is higher: their existing SS benefit or their UBI floor amount. New entrants transition to personal investment accounts. Mid-career workers receive a prorated blend during the transition period (see Transition Plan document).

### 10.3 What Companies Gain
