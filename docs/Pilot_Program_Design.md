# Pilot Program Design: The Four Pillars UBI Framework
### From Theory to Testing
**Author:** Rob DeMaria | **Version:** 1.0 | **Date:** April 18, 2026

---

## Purpose

Before national rollout, the Four Pillars framework should be tested in a controlled pilot to validate economic assumptions, refine administrative systems, measure behavioral responses, and build political support through demonstrated results.

---

## Pilot Structure

The pilot operates as a federal demonstration project authorized by Congress (similar to welfare reform waivers or ACA state innovation waivers under Section 1332). Duration: 3 years minimum, 5 years ideal. Target: 50,000-100,000 adults in a defined geographic area.

**What gets tested:** The floor payment, Pillar 2-4 qualification and payment systems, VAT impact on local businesses, work behavior changes, healthcare gap coverage mechanics, and 401(k) enrollment and contribution patterns.

**What doesn't get tested at pilot scale:** Automation tax (requires national corporate reporting), full VAT (can simulate with supplemental payments), macroeconomic effects (GDP, inflation — too small to measure locally), Social Security transition.

---

## Site Selection Criteria

The ideal pilot city meets several requirements. It should be mid-sized (metro population 200K-500K) to balance statistical power with manageable scale. The local economy should be diverse — not dependent on a single employer or industry — to test across occupation types. The demographic profile should roughly mirror national averages in income distribution, racial composition, age distribution, and urban/suburban/rural mix. The city needs cooperative state and local government willing to partner on implementation and data sharing. It should have an existing social services infrastructure that can be adapted rather than built from scratch. And it shouldn't already be running another major UBI or cash transfer experiment.

**Candidate cities (illustrative, not exhaustive):**

Dayton, Ohio is a strong candidate. Metro population around 810K provides scale, the economy is diversified across manufacturing, healthcare, education, and defense, income distribution is close to national median, and state government has shown openness to policy innovation. The region's experience with manufacturing decline also makes it relevant to the automation displacement thesis.

Tulsa, Oklahoma offers a different political environment. Conservative-leaning state tests the "no income tax" messaging, the economy spans energy, aerospace, healthcare, and tech, and the George Kaiser Family Foundation's existing social investment infrastructure provides partnership potential.

Richmond, Virginia balances geography and politics. As a state capital it offers government partnership access, the economy is diversified, demographics closely mirror national averages, and the proximity to DC simplifies federal oversight.

Boise, Idaho represents a rapidly growing western city. Its tight labor market tests whether the floor affects work participation in low-unemployment environments, the political environment is conservative (testing right-of-center messaging), and it has a simpler regulatory environment.

## Pilot Components

**Component 1: Floor Payments.** Every adult US citizen in the pilot area receives the phased floor payment — $25K/year for those earning under $30K, phasing out at $80K. Funded entirely by the federal pilot budget. Payments are monthly ($2,083 max). Enrollment is automatic for residents who file taxes in the area; manual enrollment for non-filers through a simplified application.

**Component 2: Pillar 2-4 Qualification.** Residents can apply for Creative, Civic, and Family pillar payments using the tiered system. This tests the measurement and verification infrastructure: organization partnerships, attestation systems, quality metrics, and fraud detection. Payments scale with tier level as defined in the calibration model.

**Component 3: Tax Simulation.** Since a city can't implement VAT or eliminate federal income tax, the pilot simulates the net tax impact through supplemental payments. Each participant receives an additional monthly payment equal to the estimated net benefit of the tax restructuring for their income level (income tax + FICA savings minus estimated VAT burden). This isn't perfect but lets participants experience the actual take-home income they'd have under the full system.

**Component 4: Healthcare Gap Coverage.** Uninsured and underinsured pilot participants receive healthcare coverage through a federal pilot insurance plan. This tests the "employer insurance stays, government covers gaps" model at local scale. Track enrollment, utilization, costs, and health outcomes.

**Component 5: 401(k) Enrollment.** All employed pilot participants are automatically enrolled in a pilot 401(k) program with 10% mandatory contribution. Employers in the pilot area receive incentives to provide matching. Track contribution compliance, investment choices, and participant attitudes toward personal retirement accounts vs. Social Security.

## Budget

For a pilot serving 75,000 adults over 3 years:

Floor payments represent the largest cost. At an average payment of $18,000/person (blended across full and partial recipients, since not all earn under $30K), annual floor cost is approximately $1.35B. Pillar 2-4 payments average roughly $5,000 per qualifying participant, with an estimated 30,000 participants at a cost of $150M/year. Tax simulation supplements average $8,000/person across the pilot population, adding $600M/year. Healthcare gap coverage for approximately 25,000 uninsured participants at $12,000/person costs $300M/year. 401(k) administration and government matching contribution adds approximately $50M/year. Research, evaluation, and administration costs total $75M/year.

Total annual pilot budget: approximately $2.5B. Three-year total: $7.5B. Five-year total: $12.5B.

For context, the federal government spent $6.4T in 2024. This pilot represents 0.04% of annual federal spending, roughly equivalent to 3 days of defense spending or the cost of two Virginia-class submarines.

## Evaluation Framework

**Primary Outcomes (Year 1-3):**

Employment and work behavior are measured through labor force participation, hours worked, job changes, entrepreneurship rates, and labor market mobility. The key question is whether the floor reduces workforce participation or, as hypothesized, enables better job matching and entrepreneurship.

Financial stability is tracked through emergency savings, debt levels, credit scores, housing stability, food security, and healthcare utilization. These measure whether the floor achieves its basic purpose of eliminating poverty.

Pillar participation measures enrollment rates, tier distribution, verification system performance, and fraud rates for Pillars 2-4. This validates the measurement and verification infrastructure.

Local economic impact tracks business formation, retail spending, commercial vacancy rates, property values, and tax revenue. This measures whether UBI-driven consumer spending stimulates local economic growth.

**Secondary Outcomes (Year 2-5):**

Health and wellbeing metrics include physical and mental health outcomes, substance abuse rates, domestic violence incidents, and child welfare indicators. Educational outcomes track enrollment in education and training programs, completion rates, and skills acquisition. Social cohesion is measured through civic participation, crime rates, community organization membership, and reported social trust. Administrative efficiency tracks program cost per participant, fraud detection rates, and system uptime and reliability.

**Control Group Design:** The pilot uses a randomized controlled trial (RCT) where possible, with a comparison city (similar demographics, no pilot) providing additional controls. Within the pilot city, a subset of eligible residents is randomly assigned to a waitlist control group (receiving payments starting in Year 2) to measure initial impact. The comparison city receives enhanced data collection but no pilot payments.

## Rollback Provisions

Each component can be independently scaled back or terminated. Floor payments can be reduced (from $25K to $20K to $15K) or population can be narrowed. Pillar 2-4 payments can be suspended independently. Tax simulation supplements can be adjusted quarterly. Healthcare coverage has a 6-month wind-down provision to avoid abrupt coverage loss.

A full pilot termination requires 12 months notice, 6-month payment wind-down, and transition support for participants. This mirrors the responsible rollback provisions in the national Transition Plan.

---

## Implementation Timeline

**Months 1-6 (Pre-Launch):** Congressional authorization, site selection, partnership agreements with state and local government, research institution partnership (university-based evaluation team), administrative system design and testing, community engagement and enrollment planning.

**Months 7-12 (Soft Launch):** Begin enrollment, start floor payments to first cohort (10,000 participants), launch Pillar 2-4 qualification system in beta, begin healthcare gap coverage enrollment, initialize 401(k) enrollment with pilot employers.

**Months 13-18 (Full Launch):** Scale to full 75,000 participant enrollment, all five components operational, first interim data analysis, public reporting of initial outcomes.

**Months 19-36 (Operations + Evaluation):** Continuous program operation, quarterly data reporting, Year 2 interim evaluation, system refinements based on operational learning, Year 3 comprehensive evaluation report.

**Months 37-60 (Extension if warranted):** Continue operations with any refinements, final comprehensive evaluation, policy recommendations for national scaling, transition planning for either expansion or wind-down.

## Political Strategy for Authorization

The pilot is positioned as fiscal responsibility — testing before committing trillions. This appeals to deficit hawks who want evidence before large-scale spending changes.

For conservatives: the pilot tests eliminating income tax and replacing government-controlled Social Security with personal investment accounts. It's a market-based experiment. No new permanent entitlements are created — it's a time-limited demonstration.

For progressives: the pilot provides guaranteed income, healthcare, and retirement security to a community that needs it. It measures real outcomes for real families. It creates evidence for the policies progressives want to scale nationally.

For moderates: this is how responsible policy is made. Test the theory, measure the results, adjust before scaling. No other UBI proposal has offered to prove itself before asking for national implementation.

Potential legislative vehicle: a standalone bill or an amendment to an appropriations bill. Bipartisan sponsorship is essential — ideally a fiscal conservative and a progressive Democrat.

---

## Success Criteria

The pilot is considered successful if it demonstrates: no significant reduction in labor force participation (within 5 percentage points of baseline), measurable improvement in financial stability for bottom-quartile participants, Pillar 2-4 measurement system operational with fraud rate below 3%, local business activity maintained or increased, and participant satisfaction above 70%.

The pilot is considered a basis for national scaling if, in addition: employment quality improves (better job matching, higher job satisfaction), health outcomes improve for healthcare gap participants, 401(k) contributions sustained at or above mandatory minimums, local government reports net positive fiscal impact (reduced emergency services, reduced welfare administration), and the evaluation team's cost-benefit analysis shows positive return.
